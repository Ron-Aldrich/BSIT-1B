Rizal Law (RA 1425)

A quick reviewer: learn, flip, quiz.

What is RA 1425?

Republic Act No. 1425, popularly known as the Rizal Law, requires all educational institutions to include courses on the life, works and writings of Dr. José Rizal, particularly his novels Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo. It was enacted on June 12, 1956.

Its main purpose is to develop patriotism, nationalism, moral character and civic consciousness among Filipino students through Rizal’s ideals and sacrifices.

Official title

“An Act to Include in the Curricula of All Public and Private Schools, Colleges and Universities Courses on the Life, Works and Writings of José Rizal, Particularly His Novels Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo, Authorizing the Printing and Distribution Thereof, and for Other Purposes.”

Why was it proposed?

After World War II, many Filipino leaders were concerned that:

  • Nationalism among Filipinos was weakening.
  • The youth were less aware of their country’s history.
  • Foreign influences were becoming more dominant.
  • Many students knew little about the sacrifices of Filipino heroes.

Lawmakers believed that studying Rizal’s life and works could strengthen Filipino identity and patriotism.

The original bill

Senate Bill No. 438 was titled “An Act to Make Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo Compulsory Reading Matter in All Public and Private Colleges and Universities and For Other Purposes.”
Recto’s reasons: Rizal’s writings teach patriotism, students should understand the colonial past, and education should produce responsible, informed citizens.

Opposition to the bill

Rizal criticized the Catholic Church, so the Noli-Fili Bill met stiff opposition from Church leaders and supporters. They said the bill was an attempt to discredit the Catholic religion.

Passages they objected to

  • Abusive Spanish friars
  • Clerical corruption
  • Religious hypocrisy in the Spanish colonial period

What the Church feared

  • Students might misunderstand Catholic teachings.
  • The novels could weaken religious faith.
  • Reading the unexpurgated versions might offend Catholics.

Groups that opposed it

Catholic Action of the Philippines, Congregation of the Mission, Knights of Columbus, Catholic Teachers Guild.

The Church charged Recto with being a communist and anti-Catholic. Some Catholic schools even threatened to close if the bill passed. House Bill 5561 was likewise opposed, on grounds of constitutionality and religiosity.

The opponents’ numbers

Opponents counted passages in the novels:
  • Noli (333 pages): 25 nationalistic passages vs. 120 anti-Catholic passages.
  • Lines they found offensive to Catholic doctrine: 170 in Noli and 50 in El Fili.
These are counts claimed by the bill’s critics, not neutral facts.

“Nationalistic” samples

  • Education for progress: Ibarra’s plan for a school in San Diego.
  • Elias’ call for social reform.
  • Criticism of colonial oppression: abuse of authority, racial discrimination, unjust arrests, exploitation.

“Anti-Catholic” samples

  • Padre Dámaso’s abuse of power.
  • The desecration of Don Rafael Ibarra’s grave.
  • Padre Salvi’s hypocrisy.

Arguments of the supporters

  • Rizal criticized abuses, not religion. He opposed corruption and injustice by some religious officials, not Christianity itself.
  • Education must present historical truth. Students should learn the realities of Philippine history.
  • National interest comes first. Strengthening nationalism mattered more than shielding institutions from criticism.

The compromise: Laurel’s substitute bill

To appease the opposition, Sen. Laurel proposed a substitute bill with these changes:

  1. It covers a wider range of Rizal’s works and writings, not only the two novels.
  2. A student who objects on religious grounds may request exemption from reading the two novels.
  3. Reading the unexpurgated versions applies only to college and university students.
Exemption clause. A student who believes reading the unexpurgated novels conflicts with their religious beliefs may be exempted from reading those texts. The exemption is requested through a sworn written statement of religious belief.
But: they are not exempt from taking the Rizal course, and must study Rizal’s life and ideas through alternative materials.

Sections of RA 1425

PartWhat it says
Whereas clausesRationale: there is a need to rededicate the youth to the ideals of freedom and nationalism, and the national character is shaped by Rizal’s life and works. Reading his writings gives the youth confidence, direction, courage and determination to help the nation progress.
Section 1Courses on Rizal’s life, works and writings, particularly the Noli and Fili, are included in the curricula of all schools, colleges and universities. In college, the original or unexpurgated editions (or their English translations) are the basic texts. It also carries the religious-belief exemption.
Section 2Schools must keep an adequate number of copies of the unexpurgated novels (and other Rizal writings) in their libraries.
Section 3Rizal’s writings are made widely available through translation and printing at affordable cost, or free in some cases.
Section 4The Act does not amend or repeal Section 927 of the Administrative Code, which prohibits public school teachers from discussing religious doctrines.
Section 5Authorizes ₱300,000 from unappropriated national funds to carry out the Act.

Note: the exemption is for reading the novels on religious grounds. It does not make the whole Rizal course optional.

Goals of the Rizal Law

  1. Rededicate the lives of the youth to the ideals of freedom and nationalism for which our heroes lived and died.
  2. Pay tribute to our national hero for devoting his life and works to shaping the Filipino character.
  3. Gain an inspiring source of patriotism through the study of Rizal’s life, works and writings.

Why was Rizal chosen?

  • He advocated reform through education.
  • He used the power of writing.
  • He showed peaceful nationalism.
  • He became a symbol of freedom.
